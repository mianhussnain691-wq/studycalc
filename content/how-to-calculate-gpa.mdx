## Quick Answer

To calculate your GPA, multiply each course's credit hours by its grade point value to get grade points earned, add those up for all courses, then divide by your total credit hours. Example: a student who earns 38.4 grade points across 11 credit hours has a GPA of 3.49 (38.4 ÷ 11 = 3.49). Most US schools use a 4.0 scale.

## Why Your GPA Matters

Students who track their GPA every term catch problems early. Students who only check at the end of the semester often can't fix anything by the time they find out.

Here's why it's worth the ten minutes it takes to calculate.

**Scholarships and financial aid.** Most scholarships and grants come with a minimum GPA requirement — often 3.00, 3.20, or 3.50. Drop below it, even by a fraction of a point, and funding can be pulled. Tracking your GPA mid-semester gives you time to raise a struggling grade before it costs you.

**Graduate and professional school applications.** Programs for a Master's, PhD, MD, or JD often use GPA cutoffs to manage huge applicant pools. A 3.49 versus a 3.51 can be the line between an automatic rejection and a full review of your application.

**Job applications.** Many large employers run resumes through Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) that filter out anyone below a set GPA threshold — commonly 3.0 or 3.2. If you're close to a cutoff, knowing your exact number matters.

## What Is GPA?

**GPA (Grade Point Average)** is a number, usually between 0.0 and 4.0, that summarizes your average academic performance across a semester or term. It converts your letter grades (A, B, C, etc.) into points, weights them by how many credit hours each course was worth, and averages the result.

A GPA isn't a simple average of your letter grades. It's a **weighted average**, because not every class counts the same amount toward your total.

## Credit Hours and Grade Points, Explained

Two numbers drive every GPA calculation: **credit hours** and **grade points**.

### Credit Hours: How Much a Class Counts

Credit hours reflect how much classroom time and workload a course involves. A core lecture course might be worth 3 or 4 credit hours. A short lab or seminar might be worth just 1.

The more credit hours a class carries, the more it affects your final GPA — for better or worse.

### Grade Points: Turning Letters Into Numbers

Before you can do any math, your letter grade needs to become a number. Most schools use a standard 4.0 scale, shown below.

| Letter Grade | Percentage | Grade Point |
|---|---|---|
| A+ | 97–100% | 4.00 |
| A | 93–96% | 4.00 |
| A- | 90–92% | 3.70 |
| B+ | 87–89% | 3.30 |
| B | 83–86% | 3.00 |
| B- | 80–82% | 2.70 |
| C+ | 77–79% | 2.30 |
| C | 73–76% | 2.00 |
| C- | 70–72% | 1.70 |
| D+ | 67–69% | 1.30 |
| D | 65–66% | 1.00 |
| F | Below 65% | 0.00 |

Some schools adjust these ranges slightly, so it's worth checking your own school's grading policy if your numbers don't match exactly.

## The GPA Formula

There are two steps to the formula. Once you understand them, the whole calculation takes just a few minutes.

**Step 1 — Grade points per course:**

> Grade Points = Credit Hours × Grade Point Value

**Step 2 — Final GPA:**

> GPA = Total Grade Points ÷ Total Credit Hours

That's the entire formula. Everything below is just applying it to real numbers.

## How to Calculate GPA: Step-by-Step Example

Let's walk through a real semester with four courses.

### Step 1: List Your Courses, Credits, and Grades

| Course | Credit Hours | Letter Grade | Grade Point Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 3 | A | 4.00 |
| Physics | 4 | B | 3.00 |
| English Composition | 3 | A- | 3.70 |
| Computer Science Lab | 1 | B+ | 3.30 |

### Step 2: Calculate Grade Points Earned for Each Course

Multiply each course's credit hours by its grade point value:

- Mathematics: 3 × 4.00 = **12.0** grade points earned
- Physics: 4 × 3.00 = **12.0** grade points earned
- English Composition: 3 × 3.70 = **11.1** grade points earned
- Computer Science Lab: 1 × 3.30 = **3.3** grade points earned

### Step 3: Add Up Total Credits and Total Grade Points

- Total credit hours: 3 + 4 + 3 + 1 = **11**
- Total grade points: 12.0 + 12.0 + 11.1 + 3.3 = **38.4**

### Step 4: Divide to Find Your GPA

> GPA = 38.4 ÷ 11 = **3.49**

That's your full semester GPA, rounded to two decimal places.

## A Second Example: Two Courses

Sometimes it helps to see the formula applied to a smaller, simpler case. Say you only took two classes this term:

| Course | Credit Hours | Letter Grade | Grade Point Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemistry | 4 | B+ | 3.30 |
| History | 3 | A | 4.00 |

Grade points earned:
- Chemistry: 4 × 3.30 = 13.2
- History: 3 × 4.00 = 12.0

Total grade points: 13.2 + 12.0 = 25.2
Total credit hours: 4 + 3 = 7

> GPA = 25.2 ÷ 7 = **3.60**

Notice that even with fewer courses, the process never changes: multiply, add, divide.

## Weighted vs. Unweighted GPA

If you're comparing high school transcripts or preparing college applications, you'll run into two different GPA scales.

### Unweighted GPA

An unweighted GPA scores everything on the same 4.0 scale, no matter how hard the class was. An A in gym class counts the same as an A in AP Chemistry.

### Weighted GPA

A weighted GPA rewards harder classes by extending the scale up to 5.0:

| Course Type | Grade "A" Value | Grade "B" Value |
|---|---|---|
| AP / IB Courses | 5.00 | 4.00 |
| Honors Courses | 4.50 | 3.50 |
| Standard Courses | 4.00 | 3.00 |

If your school reports a weighted GPA, check whether the college or scholarship you're applying to wants the weighted number, the unweighted number, or both.

## Common GPA Mistakes

A few small errors can quietly throw off your calculation. Watch for these.

**Averaging grades instead of weighting them.** You can't just add letter values and divide by the number of classes. A 4-credit A and a 1-credit C don't cancel out evenly — you have to weight by credit hours.

**Forgetting that F grades still count credits.** An F earns 0.00 grade points, but the credit hours still go into your total credits attempted. That's what makes a failing grade drag your GPA down so hard.

**Mixing up Pass/Fail courses.** Pass/Fail classes usually don't carry a grade point value at all. You get the credit toward graduation, but the course is left out of your GPA math entirely.

**Letting an Incomplete turn into an F.** An "Incomplete" (I) temporarily doesn't affect your GPA. But if you miss your school's deadline to finish the work, it often converts automatically to an F.

## How to Raise Your GPA

If your GPA isn't where you want it, a few targeted moves can help.

**Focus on your high-credit courses first.** Because grade points scale with credit hours, an A in a 4-credit class raises your GPA far more than an A in a 1-credit elective.

**Use grade replacement if your school allows it.** Retaking a course you failed or did poorly in can remove the old low grade from your calculation, depending on your school's policy.

**Check the grading breakdown for each class.** If a course is 50% exams and 50% projects, and exams aren't your strength, make sure you're maximizing every point on projects and homework.

## FAQs

**What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?**
An unweighted GPA scores every class on the same 4.0 scale. A weighted GPA goes up to 5.0 and gives extra points for harder classes like AP, IB, or Honors courses. Colleges may look at either number depending on their admissions process.

**Does an F grade affect my GPA?**
Yes, significantly. An F contributes 0.00 grade points, but its credit hours still count toward your total credits attempted. That combination is what pulls your average down so much more than a low passing grade would.

**Can I raise my GPA quickly?**
The fastest way is to perform well in your highest-credit courses, since they carry more weight in the formula. Retaking a class you failed, if your school allows grade replacement, can also help a lot.

**Is GPA different from CGPA?**
Yes. GPA measures your performance in a single semester or term. CGPA (Cumulative GPA) combines every semester you've completed to show your overall performance across your whole degree. Need to calculate your overall academic performance? Try our [CGPA Calculator](https://studycalc.co).

**Do all schools use the same 4.0 scale?**
Most US schools do, but grading scales can vary slightly between institutions and countries. Always check your specific school's grading policy if your calculated GPA doesn't match your transcript.

**Is a 3.5 GPA good?**
Yes, a 3.5 GPA is generally considered strong. It falls between a B+ and A- average and meets the minimum requirement for most scholarships and many competitive graduate programs, though "good" can vary by school and major.

**Can GPA be higher than 4.0?**
On a standard unweighted scale, no — 4.0 is the maximum. But on a weighted GPA scale, which gives extra credit for AP, IB, or Honors courses, GPAs can go above 4.0, often up to 5.0.
