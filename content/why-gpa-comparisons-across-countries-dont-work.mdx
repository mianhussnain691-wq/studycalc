"Just multiply your CGPA by 25 and you'll have your percentage." "A 9.2 CGPA is basically a 4.0." You've probably seen a version of this floating around — a clean little formula that turns one country's grading system into another's.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: those formulas are real, widely used, and also **not official**. They're community conventions, not translations. Here's why the underlying systems don't actually line up as neatly as the math suggests.

## Three Scales, Three Completely Different Philosophies

- **US 4.0 GPA** is built on continuous assessment — quizzes, participation, projects, and exams all year, averaged together, with letter grades translated to points.
- **Pakistani board/university systems** are historically built around high-stakes summative exams — a smaller number of exams that carry enormous weight, often expressed as a raw percentage of total marks.
- **India's 10-point CGPA** sits somewhere in between, used at the university level, with its own grading bands that don't map cleanly to either of the other two.

These aren't three dialects of the same language. They're three different ideas about what grading is even *for* — and a formula that converts one number into another can't convert the underlying philosophy along with it.

## Why the Conventions Exist Anyway (And Why They're Still Useful)

Despite all that, the CGPA × 9.5 (India) and CGPA × 25 (Pakistan) conventions didn't come from nowhere. They're the direct linear read-out of each scale's maximum (CGPA ÷ 10 × 100, and CGPA ÷ 4 × 100), and they're genuinely useful for a quick, rough estimate — which is exactly what our [CGPA to Percentage Calculator](/cgpa-to-percentage-conversion) gives you.

But neither is backed by a single official UGC or HEC document mandating it as policy. They're the convention nearly everyone uses in practice, not a universal standard — and a few institutions use different multipliers entirely.

## Why Credential Evaluators Don't Just Use a Multiplier

This is the real tell that a simple formula isn't the full answer. Agencies like WES, which US universities and employers actually rely on for official credential evaluation, don't take your final CGPA and run it through a conversion formula at all. They evaluate your transcript **course by course, or year by year**, against the issuing institution's own documented grading scale.

Why go to that trouble instead of just multiplying? Because the same raw percentage can mean completely different things depending on:

- **How strict the specific institution's grading is** — a 70% at a notoriously rigorous program isn't the same achievement as a 70% at a lenient one.
- **Whether the scale is curved or absolute** — some systems adjust cutoffs to how the whole cohort performed; others don't.
- **What the passing threshold and grade bands actually represent locally** — a "First Division" or an "A+" carries different real-world weight depending on where it was earned.

A flat multiplier can't account for any of that. A course-by-course evaluation at least tries to.

## A Concrete Example of Why This Breaks

Imagine two students, both reporting an "85%" on paper. Student A earned it through year-round continuous assessment at a US-style institution — quizzes, homework, projects, and exams all averaged together, with plenty of room to recover from one bad week. Student B earned it on a single high-stakes board exam at the end of two years, with almost no continuous-assessment cushion and a passing bar that might sit as low as 33-40%.

Same number. Completely different risk profile, completely different grading philosophy behind it. A receiving university that just reads "85%" as "85%" and applies one flat rule to both is losing exactly the context that actually matters for judging how hard each score was to earn.

## So What Should You Actually Do With a Cross-Country GPA?

Use the quick conversion for a rough personal estimate — it's genuinely useful for that. Don't use it as if it's an official, guaranteed translation on an application, a visa form, or anything where the exact number matters legally or financially. For that, the real answer is a formal credential evaluation, not a formula.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is CGPA × 9.5 the official formula for converting Indian CGPA to percentage?
No single UGC-published document mandates this nationally. It's a widely used convention, commonly associated with CBSE and many UGC-CBCS-era universities, but some institutions use a different multiplier. Confirm with your own university for anything official.

### Is CGPA × 25 the official HEC formula for Pakistan?
No — no single HEC-published document confirming this as official policy could be verified either. It's simply the direct linear read-out of Pakistan's 4.0-scale CGPA, and the convention most students and universities use in practice.

### Does WES use these multipliers when evaluating my transcript?
No. Credential evaluators like WES generally map your individual course or year-level results against your institution's own documented grading scale, not a flat multiplier applied to your final CGPA. Don't assume a WES-reported result will match the quick-conversion estimate.

### Why can't there just be one universal GPA conversion standard?
Because grading systems reflect different educational philosophies — continuous assessment vs. exam-heavy systems, curved vs. absolute grading, different passing thresholds — not just different number scales. A single formula would have to flatten all of that away to work, which is exactly what makes it unreliable for anything official.

### If I need an exact, official conversion, what should I actually do?
Request a formal credential evaluation from a recognized agency (like WES), or contact the receiving institution's admissions or HR office directly and ask how they specifically evaluate your country's grading system. A quick-conversion calculator is a starting estimate, not a substitute for that.
