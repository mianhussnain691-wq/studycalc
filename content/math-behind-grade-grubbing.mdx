"Grade grubbing" — emailing a professor to argue for a few extra points — has a bad reputation, but almost nobody actually runs the numbers before doing it. Some of those emails are 100% worth sending. Most aren't. The difference isn't vibes, it's arithmetic, and it takes about thirty seconds to check.

## The Math You're Actually Asking About

Your grade on any one assignment doesn't move your overall course grade by the full amount — it moves it by that amount *times the assignment's weight.* A 5-point bump on something worth 10% of your course grade only moves your course grade by 0.5 points. A 5-point bump on your final exam, worth 30%, moves it by 1.5 points. Same bump, completely different payoff, because weight does most of the work.

The formula: **grade point change ≈ points gained × (assignment weight ÷ 100)**

So before you draft that email, the real question isn't "how many points am I asking for" — it's "how much is this assignment even worth."

## A Worked Example

Say you got an 85 on a quiz you think deserved an 88 — a 3-point ask. If that quiz is worth 5% of your course grade:

```
3 × (5 ÷ 100) = 0.15 points
```

That's a 0.15-point shift in your overall course percentage. It will not move your letter grade unless you're sitting on a razor's-edge boundary (like 89.9%, one-tenth of a point from an A). For almost everyone, that email is a lot of social capital spent on basically nothing.

Now flip it: the same 3-point ask on your final exam, worth 35% of your grade:

```
3 × (35 ÷ 100) = 1.05 points
```

A full point swing on your actual course grade is a completely different story — especially if you're close to a grade cutoff. Same size of disagreement with the professor, wildly different stakes, purely because of where it sits in the weighting. ## When It's Actually Worth the Conversation

Skip the vague "can I get extra credit" energy. These are the scenarios where emailing a professor is genuinely reasonable:

- **A grading error.** Points added wrong, a rubric item clearly met but marked wrong, a question graded against the wrong answer key. This isn't grade grubbing — it's just asking for a correction, and most professors expect and welcome it.
- **A borderline letter cutoff.** An 89.4% one-tenth of a point from a 90% A-cutoff is a completely reasonable thing to raise, especially if there's any rounding ambiguity in the syllabus.
- **High-weight assignments specifically.** As the math above shows, a final exam or major paper is where a legitimate disagreement is actually worth your time and the professor's — a quiz almost never is, even if the principle bothers you.

## When It's Probably Not Worth It

- **Low-weight assignments where you're arguing over a handful of points.** The math usually works out to a fraction of a single GPA decimal point — real, but rarely worth the social cost of pushing on it.
- **"I worked really hard on this" as the main argument.** Professors grade against a rubric, not effort. A grading-standard disagreement needs a grading-standard argument, not an effort argument.
- **Anything where you're already comfortably clear of every grade cutoff that matters to you.** If an extra 2 points wouldn't change your letter grade either way, there's genuinely nothing on the table.

## How to Actually Ask, If It's Worth It

If the math says it's worth raising: be specific, be brief, and point to the rubric or answer key rather than how you feel about the grade. "Can you clarify how points were allocated on question 4 against the rubric?" lands completely differently than "I think I deserve more points here." One is a question about grading standards. The other is an argument, and most professors tune those out fast.

## FAQs

### How do I find out an assignment's exact weight if the syllabus is vague?

Email your professor or TA directly and ask — it's a completely normal, low-stakes question, and most syllabi should have this listed anyway even if it's buried.

### Does asking for a grade bump ever hurt me?

Rarely, if you're professional and specific. It can hurt your standing with a professor if you do it repeatedly over trivial amounts, or if your argument is about effort rather than grading accuracy.

### What if the grading error affects multiple students, not just me?

Mention that explicitly — professors often appreciate knowing an issue is systemic rather than isolated, and it reads as you flagging a real problem rather than angling for a personal favor.

### Is it different for a final grade dispute vs a single assignment?

Final grade disputes usually go through a formal academic appeals process rather than a casual email, and typically require a grading error or documented extenuating circumstance — check your school's specific policy before assuming an email alone will work.

### Can rounding alone bump my grade (like 89.6% to 90%)?

Depends entirely on your professor's or school's stated rounding policy — some round, some truncate, some don't specify at all. This is worth clarifying from the syllabus or by asking directly, since it's a policy question, not a math argument.
